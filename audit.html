In plain English

What these numbers actually mean.

Google scores every website out of 100 in four areas. Anything under 90 is flagged as needing work. Here's where you sit and, more usefully, why it matters to someone standing in the street with a cracked phone.

Performance — 78 / 100

The headline problem is 5.0 seconds before your main content appears on a phone. Google's own research is that 53% of mobile visits are abandoned after three seconds. You are two full seconds past that line, every visit.

The good news: your blocking time is 0ms, which means the site isn't jammed — it's just carrying far more weight than it needs to. The homepage pulls 1.8MB across 36 requests and 11 separate JavaScript files, of which Google says 361KB of JavaScript and 96KB of CSS are never used at all.

Accessibility — 88 / 100

Google flags colour contrast failures, links that can only be told apart by colour, and links with no readable name for a screen reader. Seven of your thirteen homepage images have no alt text, and eleven tap targets are smaller than the recommended minimum.

This isn't only an ethics point. Roughly one in five UK adults has a disability, and contrast failures affect anyone reading a phone outdoors in daylight — which is exactly where somebody with a smashed screen is standing.

One thing worth saying up front: Best Practices is a clean 100 and SEO is 92. Whoever built this did not do a bad job for its time — the site is secure, it's responsive, it doesn't have horizontal scroll on a phone, and the written content is genuinely good. The problems below are almost all things that have drifted over the years rather than things that were built wrong. That matters, because it means most of the value here is already written; it's just buried.

Found by hand

Four faults Google can't see.

Automated tools test how a page loads. They can't tell whether the phone number on it is correct. These four were found by going through the site page by page, and they are more damaging than anything in the scores above.

1. Your main contact button dials a number that doesn't exist.

Critical
What's happening

On your Contact page, the button labelled "Call us for an instant quote" links to tel:0746391129. That is ten digits. A UK mobile number is eleven. The digit 2 is missing from the middle.

What this means for your customers

Someone lands on your Contact page — the page people go to specifically because they've decided to get in touch — taps the most obvious button on it, and their phone either fails to dial or opens a blank keypad. Most people don't try again. They go back to Google and tap the next repair shop. You never find out it happened, because a call that never connects leaves no trace.

The fix

One character. On a rebuild, every phone link on the site is generated from a single source, so this class of error can't happen again — change the number once and it changes in all thirty places.

2. Your Sat-Nav page tells customers to call the wrong number.

Critical
What's happening

The Sat-Nav repairs page says: "please call us on 07463921229". Your actual number, in the footer of that very same page, is 07463921129. One digit different.

What this means for your customers

Sat-Nav repair is one of your most distinctive services and one of the few you take in by post — meaning these are customers from outside Sheffield who found you specifically. They're the hardest visitors to earn and they're being sent to a stranger's phone. Worse, the two numbers appear within a few inches of each other, which reads to a careful customer as a business that isn't paying attention.

The fix

Same as above — contact details defined once and pulled through everywhere. Also worth adding the shop landline (0114 360 3663), which is painted on your shop window but appears nowhere on the website.

3. Your opening hours contradict themselves depending on which page you land on.

High
What's happening

There are two different sets of opening hours live on the site right now, and which one you see depends on where Google drops you.

What this means for your customers

Somebody reads "open till 5" on the homepage, finishes work at 4:15 and drives over — and you shut at 3. That is a wasted journey and, realistically, a lost customer and possibly a one-star review. The Saturday line is worse: "Closed / we may be open – call us" asks the customer to do the work of finding out whether you're trading. Most won't. And the heading above that table reads "OPENING HOURSE".

The fix

One set of hours, written once, appearing on every page — plus a live "Open now / Closed" indicator that reads the real time and tells the visitor immediately. The demo has this built and working; look at the top bar.

4. "Blog" in your main menu opens a 2017 story about a data breach.

High
What's happening

The BLOG item in your navigation doesn't go to a blog index. It links directly to a single post titled "Three Mobile hack may have affected 76,000 more customers", published around 2017. The neighbouring posts are about refurbishing an iPhone 6.

What this means for your customers

A visitor clicking a top-level menu item lands on a nine-year-old news story about customer data being stolen. It has nothing to do with your services, and it silently tells the reader the business stopped updating its website nearly a decade ago. Related: your mobile phone page still describes the iPhone XR / 11 as "the latest". That was 2019. Anyone with an iPhone 15 reads that and assumes you can't help them.

The fix

Retire the blog or replace it with something that earns its place — repair guides, "is it worth fixing?" explainers, the deep-clean walkthrough. Model numbers get written as ranges rather than named handsets, so the page doesn't age.

Search visibility

191 five-star reviews that Google can't display.

No business schema on the site

Opportunity

Your site carries generic page markup but no LocalBusiness data at all — no structured address, no opening hours, no service list, and crucially no AggregateRating. That's the code that lets Google show a gold star rating next to your listing in search results.

You have a 4.9 average from 191 reviews. That is a genuinely exceptional number for any business, and it is your single strongest asset. Right now a search result for SheffTech shows a plain blue link, the same as a shop with no reviews at all.

No H1 heading on the homepage

Opportunity

Your homepage has no H1 element anywhere on it. The H1 is the single most important on-page signal you can give a search engine about what a page is for. Yours is missing entirely, so Google is left to infer it.

Google also flags 8 links with non-descriptive text — "Learn more", "click here" — which carry no meaning for search or for screen readers. Each of those is a wasted signal pointing at a service page you'd like to rank.

Also worth knowing: Google reported "No Data" for real-user experience on your site. That means not enough people are visiting to generate a field report — which tells you the site is getting very little organic traffic relative to how well-known the shop clearly is locally. The business is doing the work; the website isn't bringing anyone in.

The bit that surprised us

Your shop window sells things your website doesn't mention.

We looked at a photograph of your shopfront. Three revenue streams are advertised in the window that appear nowhere on shefftech.co.uk.

"We Sell Used Phones"

Painted on the window. Not one word about it online. Refurbished handsets are exactly what someone searches for when a repair quote comes back higher than they hoped.

"We Buy Broken Phones"

Also on the window, also invisible online. "Sell my broken iPhone Sheffield" is a search with real volume and real intent — someone with cash in mind, ready to walk in today.

Your landline: 0114 360 3663

On the shop sign and the window vinyl. Nowhere on the website. Some customers — particularly older ones and business accounts — will simply trust a landline more than a mobile.

The same is true of your best marketing. Your TikTok has 35,900 likes and a console clean that reportedly passed a million views. On the website it's one small button near the bottom of the homepage. Meanwhile your deep-clean page contains a genuinely excellent six-stage photo walkthrough of a console strip-down — the best content you own — and almost nobody will ever find it, because it sits three clicks deep with no link from the homepage.
